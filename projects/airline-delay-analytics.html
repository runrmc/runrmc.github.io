Project Overview

Flight reliability is a core operational metric for airlines, and a key input for any travel-industry product that ranks, recommends, or predicts flight outcomes — think Expedia, Google Flights, or an airline's own ops team. This project asks a simple question with a data-heavy answer: which carriers and routes are actually reliable, what's really driving delays, and when should travelers expect the worst disruptions?

I built a SQL + Tableau analytics pipeline examining on-time performance, delay root causes, and reliability patterns across roughly 7 million U.S. domestic flights from the full 2025 calendar year, sourced from the U.S. Department of Transportation's Bureau of Transportation Statistics (BTS).

Data & Architecture

Raw monthly CSVs from BTS were cleaned with Python and pandas — trimmed from 110 columns down to 39 relevant fields, with nulls handled by business logic rather than blanket imputation — then bulk-loaded into PostgreSQL via the native COPY command for performance at scale.

The warehouse is modeled as a star schema: dim_date (one row per calendar day), dim_airport (referenced twice by the fact table, for origin and destination), dim_carrier (one row per airline), and fact_flights — roughly 7 million rows carrying foreign keys to all three dimensions plus departure/arrival performance, delay-cause breakdown, and cancellation/diversion flags.

Seven analytical SQL queries sit on top of this schema — a carrier scorecard, delay-cause breakdown, monthly and day-of-week trend analysis, route-level reliability, a carrier ranking, and a month-over-month change calculation. A few techniques stood out:

  • Aggregations with conditional logic (CASE WHEN inside SUM) to compute on-time %, cancellation %, and delay-cause share
  • A CTE combined with the LAG() window function to compute month-over-month change in on-time performance
  • RANK() for carrier reliability ranking
  • A "role-playing dimension" join pattern — dim_airport joined twice in the same query, aliased as origin and destination

Key Findings

  • Late aircraft and carrier-controllable issues, not weather, drive most delays. Late aircraft delay (39%) and carrier delay (32%) together account for over 70% of total delay-minutes; weather is only about 6%.
  • Carrier reliability varies meaningfully. On-time rates range from roughly 73% to 83%, even after controlling for cancellations.
  • Summer, not winter, is the worst season for delays. July is the single worst month — 70.8% on-time, 17.3 minute average delay — likely driven by high travel volume combined with afternoon thunderstorm season. Airlines appear to schedule more buffer in winter months in anticipation of weather.
  • Sunday and Monday are the least reliable days of the week — likely from weekend-disruption carryover, not Thursday or Friday as commonly assumed.
  • Route-level reliability reveals outliers invisible at the carrier level. Some individual routes run below 40% on-time despite their operating carrier's overall average being much higher.

The Dashboard

Query outputs and the full star-schema tables were exported to CSV and built out into an interactive Tableau Public dashboard for exploring carrier scorecards, delay-cause breakdowns, and route-level reliability.
